## What it is

A security clearance for a contractor employee begins with a request the firm may make only in narrow circumstances. Under the NISPOM rule, eligibility is requested only where access is essential to a classified contract, for the minimum number of employees, and never to build a cache of cleared staff [119]. A candidate can be investigated before employment, but only on a written commitment the candidate accepted in writing, and employment must then begin within 45 days of the grant [119]. Where nothing adverse has surfaced the cognizant agency may grant temporary eligibility, limited to one year without approval and valid at its level but not for RD, COMSEC or NATO information until the final grant; its withdrawal is not a denial and cannot be appealed [119]. No public figure says how often a contractor applicant receives an interim or how long that decision takes [127].

Behind every personnel request stands a facility clearance the firm cannot obtain by asking. A contractor cannot apply for its own entity eligibility determination; a government contracting activity or a cleared contractor sponsors it on a legitimate requirement, the firm's senior management official, facility security officer and insider-threat official must themselves hold eligibility, and foreign ownership, control or influence must not be disqualifying [120]. DCSA's roadmap puts the key officials' questionnaires and fingerprints at day 45 of the sponsorship and gives no end-to-end time [146]. A clearance already held elsewhere is meant to travel: under SEAD 7 an agency accepts another authorized agency's investigation and adjudication unless one of seven exceptions applies, such as an investigation older than seven years or an interim grant, the determination is due within five business days, and reciprocity cannot be withheld because a polygraph is wanted [122]. GAO found the practice looser than the rule, with reciprocity data inconsistent and incomplete, 28 of 31 surveyed agencies saying their systems at times lacked the information a decision needs, and contractors reporting no status updates while a transfer was delayed [145].

The decision itself applies thirteen adjudicative guidelines, from allegiance and foreign influence through financial considerations to the use of information technology, each with conditions that raise a concern and conditions that mitigate it, under the whole-person concept: "All available, reliable information about the person, past and present, favorable and unfavorable, should be considered" [123]. The Bond Amendment sits above the weighing, since an unlawful drug user or addict is ineligible with no waiver [123]. A favorable grant then opens a standing obligation. The contractor reports adverse information about cleared employees from verified sources, a duty that survives the employee's termination, along with suspicious contacts, terminations, citizenship changes and changes in its own key management personnel [189]. The employee reports under SEAD 3: unofficial foreign travel at least five days before departure, continuing foreign contacts, debts over 120 days delinquent, arrests, treatment, cohabitants and conflicting outside employment, and a failure to report can affect eligibility [190]. Industry accounted for roughly 984,000 continuous-vetting enrollments in FY2026 Q3 [126], and full enrollment is planned for September 2028 [54].

The clocks are where the rule and the plan part company. Trusted Workforce 2.0 targets are 40 days end to end for a moderate-risk position and 75 for a high-risk one; the FY2026 Q3 report put DCSA's investigation inventory at about 121,000 and rising, moved its reporting from the fastest 90 percent of cases to all cases, and said performance "remains much too high compared to performance targets" [54]. DCSA's industry figures for that quarter averaged 197 days for a Secret (21 initiation, 59 investigation, 117 adjudication) and 243 for a Top Secret (20, 97, 126), with adjudication now the longest phase [126] [125]; the last fastest-90-percent figures, for FY2026 Q1, were 156 and 227 days and cannot be compared with the new basis [124]. GAO's caveat applies to all of them: 36 of 42 timeliness statistics it reviewed were inaccurate, mostly through a DCSA calculation error that understated clearance times from 2020 to 2024 [56]. The guide records the disagreement as the controversy `clearance-timeliness-figures`. The wait has a measured cost of its own. Among 157,154 accepted entry-level offers at one telecommunications employer, 4.1 percent of hires never showed up, and each additional week between acceptance and the start date raised the odds of a no-show by about a fifth (OR 1.22), while a referral (OR 0.42) and good communication from the company (OR 0.56) lowered them [144]. That population is entry-level and customer-facing, so the rate does not transfer to cleared technical hiring; the mechanism is what the guide carries [144].

## The key idea in one picture

The clearance-clock figure is a horizontal timeline that starts at an accepted offer. Two stacked bars show DCSA's industry averages for FY2026 Q3, a Secret bar of 197 days divided into 21 days of initiation, 59 of investigation and 117 of adjudication, and a Top Secret bar of 243 days divided 20, 97 and 126 [126]. Two short reference marks above the bars show the 40-day and 75-day targets [54], and a third mark at about 45 days shows the median time to fill a role [43]. At the right-hand end of each bar a bracket of 45 days shows the period within which a pre-employment hire must start after the grant [119], and a footnote says the published statistics understated real processing time from 2020 to 2024 [56].
