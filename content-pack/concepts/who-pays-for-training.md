## What it is

Economists divide what a worker learns into general human capital, skills any employer can use, and specific human capital, skills worth something only at the firm that taught them. The standard theory, as Acemoglu and Pischke restate it, says workers pay for general training because they can take it anywhere, so a firm should fund only firm-specific skills [161]. Their 1998 paper offers a theory in which workers do not pay for the general training they receive: the current employer knows the trained worker's ability better than rival employers do, that information advantage gives it ex post monopsony power, and the power makes paying for general skills worthwhile; German apprentice data give the predictions some support [161]. For a small firm the point is that it trains because it knows what the trained person can do [161].

The return arrives as a wedge between what the trained person produces and what they are paid. On a panel of British industries from 1983 to 1996, a one percentage point increase in training was associated with an increase of about 0.6 percent in value added per hour and about 0.3 percent in hourly wages, so output rose about twice as much as pay and the firm kept the difference while the person stayed [159]. Part of what a firm spends is not a course at all. On German establishment data, workers with higher-paid coworkers had faster wage growth, and between 4 and 9 percent of total compensation took the form of learning from more knowledgeable coworkers [158]; for a firm with one senior per specialty that is what mentoring costs in billable hours, which is the constraint `c-mentoring-capacity-is-senior-billable-time` [158] [99].

Paying for education keeps people, but mostly by changing who applies. At one large establishment that adopted a tuition-reimbursement program, retention rose, and 80 percent of the program's overall effect came through sorting, who chose to join the firm, with the remainder through participation [160]. Whether a course changes anything on the job is a separate question. The transfer-of-training review finds the most consistent support for realistic training environments, behavioral modeling and error management in the design, a supervisor who expects the skill to be used (ρ .31, against .14 for peer support), a transfer climate (.37) and an early opportunity to perform, with motivation to transfer at .43 against .07 for motivation to learn; the often-repeated estimate that only 10 percent of training spending transfers is a 1982 figure, not a measurement [162].

The instruments a firm once used to hold the investment are narrowing. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau treats training repayment provisions as employer-driven debt: nearly 10 percent of American workers surveyed in 2020 were covered by one, 44.8 percent of nurses with five years or less, with repayment demands documented far above the training's cost, and the research the Bureau cites puts a training contract's effect at around 15 percent less quitting [166].

California's AB 692 makes it unlawful, for contracts entered after January 1, 2026, to require repayment of training, education, relocation, signing or retention amounts for leaving before a date, with exceptions for a transferable credential under a separate, prorated, at-cost agreement and for signing bonuses on stated conditions, no repayment on a termination except for misconduct, and a $5,000 penalty or actual damages [167]. Colorado permits repayment only for training distinct from normal on-the-job training, at reasonable cost declining proportionally over two years, and Connecticut bars promissory notes as a condition of employment [168]. New York's Trapped at Work Act, signed December 19, 2025 and amended February 13, 2026, makes employment promissory notes unenforceable from February 13, 2027, with exceptions for bonuses and tuition toward a transferable credential, and fines of $1,000 to $5,000 per violation [212] [211]. Washington's noncompete statute, effective June 30, 2027, treats repayment and forfeiture terms tied to working in one's field as void noncompetes and leaves outside the ban a written agreement to repay out-of-pocket educational expenses that expires within 18 months of the start date [210]. The federal positions moved the other way: the NLRB general counsel's memoranda against stay-or-pay terms were rescinded and the FTC rule that would have treated some provisions as noncompetes is not in effect [168].

On the cost side the rules are permissive. Training and education related to the field in which the employee works or may reasonably be expected to work are allowable costs, except overtime for training, salaries for attending undergraduate or part-time graduate classes in working hours absent unusual circumstances, full-time graduate education beyond two school years, and training for other than bona fide employees [175]. Up to $5,250 per employee per year of educational assistance is excludable from wages under a separate written plan with notice to employees, covering tuition, fees, books and, since March 27, 2020 and now without an end date, principal and interest on the employee's own education loans; the amount is indexed for taxable years after 2026 [174].

The guide holds these findings together in the controversy `who-pays-for-training`, with five positions: the standard theory, the information-advantage account, the productivity–wage wedge, the sorting result, and the regulators' view that repayment provisions shift cost and risk to the worker and chill mobility whether or not enforced [161] [159] [160] [166] [167] [168]. Its reading, marked as synthesis, is that the return comes through the wedge while the person stays and through the applicants the offer attracts, not through a repayment clause [159] [160] [166].
