## What it is

<!-- framing -->

A gate is the guide's record for a rule that binds an opening regardless of evidence: a statute, a regulation, a contract clause or an agency directive. Every gate carries the same fields. The `what` field states what the rule requires, each sentence cited to the governing text; `governing_text` names the statute or clause; `applies_when` gives the threshold or situation that brings the rule into play; `binds` lists the stages where it bites; `steps` are the actions the governing source itself describes; `pending_changes` holds dated entries with a status; and `conflicts` says where the sources disagree or where a fact rests on something weaker than a primary. The gate `nispom-eligibility-and-sponsorship` shows the shape: its governing text is 32 CFR 117.10, it applies to any contractor in the National Industrial Security Program requesting eligibility for an employee, it binds four stages, and its steps follow the rule's own terms, including the start within 45 days of a grant for a hire investigated before employment [119].

A federal-contractor gate applies in every state, and the state record is laid over it. Each state record, one per state and the District of Columbia, carries nine overlays, pay-range posting, salary-history bans, noncompetes, stay-or-pay terms, AI in hiring, cannabis testing, fair chance, credit checks and mini-WARN notice, each with a status, cited text, the federal gate it modifies and a flag saying whether a primary source sits behind it. Every one of the nine state protections for off-duty cannabis use or against pre-employment THC testing excepts federal contracts, federal funding or clearance work, so a protection that binds an ordinary employer stops at the edge of a cleared position [219]. Several state credit-check limits likewise except positions that require a security clearance, which the FCRA gate records beside the federal notice duties that still apply [218]. The federal gate sets the floor and the overlay adds duties or exceptions on top of it.

A change enacted but not yet in force is recorded apart from current law, so today's rule is not confused with next year's. The changes file dates each entry and gives it a status from a fixed vocabulary, proposed, announced, class-deviation, final, pending-implementation, suspended or withdrawn, and a superseded entry is marked, not deleted. Colorado is the clearest example. The 2024 AI Act never took effect: a stipulated order of April 27, 2026 barred its enforcement, and SB 26-189, signed May 14, 2026, repealed and reenacted it as a notice-based law applying from January 1, 2027 [221] [222]. The legislature's fiscal note describes the duties from that date, notice before automated decision-making technology materially influences an employment decision, a description within 30 calendar days of an adverse outcome, meaningful human review, records kept at least three years, and Attorney General enforcement after a 60-day cure notice [222]. The gate `ai-screening-laws` carries that as a pending change dated to 2027, and the entry that gave the old Act a 2026 effective date is marked superseded [222] [221]. Washington is the other primary-sourced case. ESHB 1155 makes every noncompetition covenant void and unenforceable from June 30, 2027 regardless of when it was signed, sweeps repayment and forfeiture terms tied to working in one's field into the same category, keeps nonsolicitation terms with the customer restriction capped at 18 months, and requires notice to affected workers by October 1, 2027 [210]. That one statute appears under the noncompete, stay-or-pay and no-poach gates and under the Washington state record [210].

<!-- framing -->

The last thing to read in a gate is where its facts came from. The guide's standing rule is that law-firm, consultant and vendor commentary never carries a fact alone; it points at a primary source or flags a change, and where a gate rests on such a source its `conflicts` line says so and the primary fetch is logged as a todo. The gate `training-repayment-and-stay-or-pay` states that the California, New York, Connecticut, Colorado and Wyoming terms rest on law-firm summaries while Washington's rests on the statute [210]. The state file makes the same admission: its header says the overlays rest on dated compilations, names the session laws and the fiscal note as the only primaries read in that sweep, and explains that `primary_pending: true` means no primary is among an overlay's sources, which the app renders as compilation-sourced, to be verified. A status of `none-found` means the state is absent from the named compilation as of its date, not that the state's law was checked.

## The key idea in one picture

<!-- framing -->

The state-overlay-counts figure is a row of nine horizontal bars, one per overlay. Each bar is divided by status in the overlay's own vocabulary, so the noncompete bar separates void, void-from-2027, income-threshold, category-limits and common-law. A hatched fill marks the segments whose records are compilation-sourced, and the few solid segments, the overlays with a primary behind them, stand out by their rarity. A caption under the cannabis bar notes that every statewide protection shown excepts federal contract and clearance work [219], so the bar reads as a map of where the overlay exists, not of where it reaches a cleared opening.

## How this guide uses it

<!-- framing -->

Gates appear on every stage record, in the `gates` list naming the rules that bind that stage, with the clear-start stage carrying the most; on constraint and lever records that name the rule the constraint runs into or the lever must respect; and in the pathfinder, whose state question selects the state record, after which the node panel for a gate lists the overlays that point at it. The nine overlays modify `pay-transparency-posting-laws`, `salary-history-bans`, `noncompete-agreements-federal-status` with `no-poach-and-non-solicitation`, `training-repayment-and-stay-or-pay`, `ai-screening-laws` with `applicant-record-retention`, `drug-free-workplace`, `fair-chance-act-contractors`, `fcra-background-checks` and `warn-act`. The dated changes behind the examples above are `ch-027` for Colorado [222] [221] and `ch-028` for Washington [210], each listing the gates and state records it touches.
