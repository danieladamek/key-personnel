# Key Personnel: what constrains filling and keeping an opening at a small technical firm whose hires must satisfy a contract or a customer

<!-- section: abstract -->
## Abstract

A small technical firm that sells its people's time to a government customer does not hire the way the textbooks assume. The opening is real only when a contract funds it, the salary is bounded by a labor-category rate that was fixed at award [98] [99], the person may need a clearance that only a contract need can justify and that takes months the firm does not control [119] [126], and the customer may accept or reject the individual by name [197] [203]. This guide maps that situation stage by stage, from planning an opening to handing a role over, and asks at each stage what the evidence says about the levers a firm can pull. The selection evidence is unusually clear: structured interviews, job-knowledge tests and work samples predict performance, unstructured interviews and years of experience barely do, and the most valid tests carry the largest group differences [1]. The retention evidence is a ranked table in which wanting to leave and looking come first and pay sits well down the list [178]. The gates have moved: the contractor affirmative-action framework was rescinded in 2026 [86], the federal noncompete rule was vacated and abandoned [169], four states and one city regulate algorithmic screening [38] [220], and three states added pay-range posting laws this year [223] [224]. Every constraint, lever and gate is a record with its own citations; every dependency between them is an edge with its own evidence; nothing is recommended, only ranked by what fits the opening. Written by an AI research builder from 235 sources read as of 22 October 2026; not peer reviewed; not advice.

## How to read this guide

<!-- framing -->
The guide is built from records, and the main text is the reading order for them. Nine stages run across the middle of Figure 5: plan, attract, select, offer, clear and start, onboard, develop, retain, hand over. Each stage has a record that says who decides what there, which failure patterns are seen at it, which metrics measure it and which gates bind it. Underneath the stages sit sixty constraints, the things that bind an opening, each with the mechanism, how to tell it is the one in play, the levers that act on it and the gates it touches. Sixty-nine levers carry an evidence grade, the effect as the study reported it, the population it was measured in, and a line on what the lever has not been shown to do. Forty-seven gates carry their governing text and their pending changes. The dependency graph on the map page draws 144 edges, each with its own cited evidence, and infers nothing.

The stages come from the literatures the guide draws on, which organise hiring as a sequence from attraction through selection to socialization and retention rather than as a set of departments [152] [178] [14]. The decisions at each stage are split among parties the firm does not control. A clearance can be requested only for a classified contract the person will work on, never to build a bench, and a candidate investigated before employment must start within 45 days of the grant [119]. A time-and-materials labor category carries a fixed hourly rate that already contains wage, indirect cost and profit, and applies only to labor that meets the category's qualifications [98] [99]. A customer may set the qualifications and accept or reject the product, but a contract under which the government supervises the contractor's people is a personal-services contract the rules forbid [197]. The stage records say which of these parties decides at each step, and the levers are the part the firm keeps.

<!-- framing -->
The front page is a pathfinder. It asks what you are seeing, which stage the opening is at if you know, which conditions describe the role (a clearance, a customer who approves by name, an urgent start, a rare skill, pay above the rate, a possible junior hire, a tentative opening, tight cash, an overloaded team, a fixed onsite schedule) and which state the work is in. It then ranks the constraints by a count of matches: three points when the pattern you named points at the constraint, two when the stage matches, one for each condition. Nothing is recommended. The ranked set is the narrowed field, and the map page highlights it against the full graph so that the dependencies between the constraints you are facing and the ones you are not can be walked in both directions.

Every figure in the records is as its source reported it, with the population named where the study was one firm or one sector. The first-year output ramp of 53 percent comes from windshield installers at one autoglass company [157]; the 4.1 percent of accepted offers that never started comes from entry-level hires at one telecommunications company, where each extra week between acceptance and start raised the odds of a no-show with an odds ratio of 1.22 [144]; the 1.7-year mean stay after an accepted counteroffer is a staffing firm's survey of senior managers [113]. The guide carries these as the best measured figures available and says what they were measured on, because a recruiter at a cleared engineering firm is not hiring windshield installers.

## 1. What the evidence says predicts a good hire, and what the law allows you to ask

<!-- figure: validity-2022 -->

The `select` stage runs from the first screen of applications to the scored decision about who gets an offer,
the question a centenary review names applied psychology's Supreme Problem, open since 1917 [26]. The firm
chooses the method and the decision rule; the law fixes what may be asked and when [33] [34]; where a customer
approves the person, its standard sits on top of the firm's, within the limits the plan stage describes [197].
Two failure patterns belong here: `many-apply-few-qualify`, where the screen passes almost no one [44], and
`withdraw-mid-process`, where candidates leave before a decision [16].

Figure 1 shows the 2022 re-estimation of how well each common method predicts job performance, as operational
validity [1]. Structured interviews lead at .42, then job-knowledge tests at .40, empirically keyed biodata at
.38, work samples at .33 and cognitive ability at .31; unstructured interviews sit at .19 and years of job
experience at .07 [1]. The figures are below the 1998 estimates that put general mental ability and the
structured interview both at .51 [4], because the earlier meta-analyses overcorrected for range restriction in
concurrent studies [1]. A rebuttal argues the restriction is larger than assumed [2], and the size of the
cognitive-ability coefficient is the `cognitive-ability-validity` controversy in chapter 8. In both sets of
estimates a structured interview, a knowledge test and a work sample each predict well [1] [4], and the
cognitive test carries the largest group difference of any common predictor [1].

How the evidence is combined matters about as much as what is collected. Across nine job-performance
comparisons, the same information combined by formula reached r .44 against .28 by judgment; the authors'
conclusion was that "data combination is best done mechanically while information collection can be done quite
effectively by experts" [7]. Across fifteen service-sector firms that introduced a pre-employment test, managers
who overrode it made worse hires on average [10]. Interviewers in the laboratory formed confident impressions
from random answers, and adding an interview halved their predictive accuracy [8]. Among 131 professional
interviewers, formal training was the modifiable antecedent of using the structure components that carry
validity [19]. The levers are `l-structured-interview`, `l-mechanical-scoring` and `l-interviewer-training`, and
OPM publishes a free guide to the method [41].

The first screen is where qualified people are lost. In the employer survey behind the Hidden Workers report, 88
percent of employers said qualified high-skilled candidates were rejected outright for not matching criteria
exactly, and 94 percent said so for middle-skilled candidates; the remedy proposed is affirmative filters that
search for required skills [44]. This is `c-keyword-screen-rejects-qualified`, with
`l-affirmative-skill-filters` as its lever; whether announced moves to skills-based hiring changed anything is
the `skills-based-hiring` controversy in chapter 8.

Work samples and knowledge tests are the valid replacements for the proxy, on the conditions the evidence
summaries attach: a job analysis, standardized administration and scoring, and use for prerequisites that will
not be trained [17]. Format matters for software roles. In a randomized trial of 48 computer-science students,
solving the same problem on a whiteboard while watched halved performance and raised measured stress relative to
solving it privately [21], and developers on a large forum describe live coding as unrelated to real work [22].
That is `c-technical-interview-measures-anxiety`; `l-private-or-collaborative-technical-assessment` keeps the
exercise a sample of the job and changes the setting, on the strength of that one small trial [21].

Validity has a price in group differences, and the same paper pairs each coefficient with it. Cognitive-ability
tests carry a Black–White standardized difference of .79, work samples .67, job-knowledge tests .54, structured
interviews .23 and integrity tests .10, with the caveat that the d values mix applicant, incumbent and national
samples [1]. Even the interview's figure is qualified: corrected for the range restriction of using the
interview as a first screen, two behavioral interview forms showed d of .36 and .56 [18]. The trade-off is not
automatic, though: when a national retailer replaced informal screening with a standardized test across 1,363
stores, median tenure rose by more than 10 percent and minority hiring did not fall, because the informal screen
was no less biased [11]. The constraint is `c-validity-diversity-tradeoff`; `l-adverse-impact-monitoring` and
`l-pareto-weighting` are its levers.

Applicants' views of the process predict whether they stay in it. Across 86 samples and 48,750 people,
interviews (3.84 on a five-point scale) and work samples (3.63) were rated most favorably and personality tests
(2.88) and honesty tests (2.47) least; perceptions correlated with organizational attractiveness, intention to
accept an offer and willingness to recommend the employer, and face validity drove the fairness judgments [14].

Slow and burdensome processes lose people. In the one field study of offer timing, 3,012 archival records at one
company, earlier post-interview offers were more likely to be accepted, and the people hired on fast offers
performed and stayed no differently [15]. Among 3,550 police applicants in a multiple-hurdle process, those who
withdrew differed from those who stayed in how they saw the organization and the process, with different reasons
at different stages [16]. SHRM's 2025 benchmarks put the median time-to-fill at about 45 days, with 41 percent
of organizations reporting rising candidate ghosting [43]. No primary study of interview rounds against
withdrawal was found, so `c-process-burden-withdrawal` is supported in mechanism and unmeasured in magnitude,
while `c-slow-decisions-lose-candidates` has the field study [15] [16]. The levers are
`l-fast-decision-and-offer`, `l-interview-plan-and-preview` and `l-realistic-job-preview`, the last of which
lowered attrition during recruiting across 40 studies [12].

The law starts with the Uniform Guidelines of 1978 (`ugesp`) [30]. An employer keeps records of selection rates
by sex and by race or ethnic group; a group's rate below four-fifths of the highest group's is generally
regarded as evidence of adverse impact, qualified by statistical and practical significance and by small numbers
[31]. A procedure with adverse impact needs criterion-related, content or construct validity evidence tied to
important elements of job performance, and more evidence for ranking than for pass/fail use [32]. The EEOC's
2007 fact sheet adds that such a procedure must be job-related and consistent with business necessity and that
the employer remains responsible for a vendor's test (`eeoc-selection-procedures`) [33]. Without this evidence a
firm is in `c-unvalidated-criteria`; `l-validate-and-document-screen` is the remedy, measured against the SIOP
Principles of 2018 [29].

The ADA sets the sequence of questions (`ada-pre-offer-inquiries`). Before a conditional offer an employer may
not ask disability-related questions or require medical examinations; it may ask whether the applicant can
perform job functions with or without reasonable accommodation, and about licenses, attendance and arrest or
conviction records [34]. After a conditional offer, medical questions and examinations are allowed if required
of everyone entering the job category [34].

Automated screens carry the same rules plus new ones (`ai-screening-laws`). On May 18, 2023 the EEOC stated that
an algorithmic tool used to make or inform a selection decision is a selection procedure under the Uniform
Guidelines and that the employer answers for a vendor's tool; it removed the document on January 27, 2025, and
the law it restated did not change [35] [36]. The duties now sit with states and one city. New York City
requires a bias audit no more than a year old, a published summary and candidate notice, with penalties of $500
to $1,500 a day [37]; Illinois requires notice from January 1, 2026 [38]; California's regulations, effective
October 1, 2025, extend liability to agents, treat anti-bias testing as a defense factor and require four-year
retention of records [40]; Colorado's reenacted act requires notice, an explanation after an adverse outcome and
human review from January 1, 2027 [222] [221]. The constraint is `c-algorithmic-screen-exposure`.

Other rules time or bar particular questions. Since December 20, 2021 a federal contractor may not ask about
criminal history for positions within the scope of a federal contract until after a conditional offer, with
exceptions for positions requiring access to classified information, law-enforcement or national-security
duties, and cases where a law requires the inquiry earlier (`fair-chance-act-contractors`, read so far in a
law-firm summary) [48]; the EEOC's 2012 guidance asks for a targeted screen with an individualized assessment
[47]. And a posting for ITAR- or EAR-controlled work may not be limited to U.S. citizens, or to citizens and
permanent residents, because the export rules' definition of a U.S. person includes nationals, refugees and
asylees; settlements in 2023 ran from $365,000 to
$500,000 per employer (`citizenship-status-export-control`)
[49] [50]. DOJ's 2023 fact sheet says to keep export assessments separate from Form I-9 [51]. These rules are
`c-premature-inquiries`; `l-conditional-offer-sequencing` moves each reserved question to the far side of the
offer.

<!-- synthesis -->

Put together, the stage has an unusually consistent answer: structure beats judgment by a wide margin [1] [7],
the most valid tests carry the largest group differences [1], applicants prefer interviews and work samples
[14], and slow or opaque processes lose people [15] [16]. A firm that builds a structured interview and a
job-relevant work sample from a job analysis, scores them by formula, keeps the selection-rate records, and asks
the reserved questions only after a conditional offer is on the right side of both positions in the validity
dispute and of every gate in this chapter [1] [4] [31] [34] [48].

## 2. Where candidates come from

The `attract` stage is where the opening reaches people who could fill it, through the posting, the channels,
the firm's own networks and the pool the law allows. The market sets the pool and the going terms [71] [84]; the
firm decides the posting, the channels, the referral program and the work arrangement [65] [60] [70]; the law
decides the words, the listing duty and who may be excluded [78] [73] [49]; the customer decides whether the
work can be remote or uncleared. The failure pattern is `few-plausible-candidates` [71].

The pool a small firm sees is thin by its own account. Among NFIB members hiring or trying to hire in September
2026, 87 percent reported few or no qualified applicants and 32 percent of all owners had unfilled openings
[71]. BLS projects about 280,000 openings a year in computer and IT occupations over 2025–35, at a group median
wage of $109,470 in May 2025 [84]. An outplacement firm's quarterly survey found 2.7 percent of job seekers
relocating for a new job in the second quarter of 2024, against an annual average of 11.2 percent in 2017 [234],
and surveyed workers assign a mean value of 8 percent of pay to a two-or-three-day work-from-home option [70].
This is `c-local-pool-thin`.

<!-- synthesis -->

The lever those figures point to, `l-remote-or-hybrid-option`, is the one a cleared or site-bound opening cannot
pull, because the customer's access rules decide it. A firm that must have people onsite competes for the local
pool against employers offering the 8 percent differential in kind, and pays it in cash or accepts a smaller
pool [70] [234].

Of the sources, referral has the best evidence. Across nine large firms in three industries, referred applicants
were substantially more likely to be hired and referred workers were 10 to 30 percent less likely to quit (about
13 percent in call centers, 12 percent in trucking, 30 percent in high-tech), and referrals from above-median
workers were worth far more than those from below-median workers [60]. In one financial-services firm with
62,127 applications, referred applicants were 7.3 percentage points more likely to be interviewed and about 15
percent less likely to leave [61]. The same mechanism narrows the pool: linked employer–employee data from
Brazil show new firms more likely to hire, and less likely to dismiss, workers who share the founder's race, the
difference fading only as cumulative hires grow, because referral networks are segregated; the mechanism
transfers, the estimates do not [81].

<!-- synthesis -->

So `l-employee-referral-program` is both the best channel and, under `c-referral-network-narrow`, the smallest;
the Uniform Guidelines' selection-rate records by group will show the effect over time [31] [81].

What a posting draws is set mostly by two things in the ad. In data from a large job board, titles carry most of
the information, so across titles higher-paying postings draw fewer applicants, while within a title "jobs with
10% higher wages do attract 7.7% more applicants" [65]. The range is now the norm: by May 2025, 59 percent of US
postings and 63 percent of software-development postings on the largest job board carried a salary figure [75].
A law-firm map of early 2026 counts thirteen states and the District of Columbia with posting-range laws [76];
Virginia (July 1, 2026), Maine (July 29, 2026) and Connecticut (October 1, 2026) were added this year, and
Delaware's takes effect on September 26, 2027 (`pay-transparency-posting-laws`) [223] [224]. Across 71 studies,
job and organization characteristics, recruiter behavior, process perceptions, fit and hiring expectancy predict
attraction, and recruiter demographics do not [63]; employer images form through recruiters and word of mouth
[64]. The constraints are `c-posting-draws-wrong-applicants` and `c-employer-image-weak`; the levers are
`l-posting-rewrite` and `l-employer-image-management`.

Words and delivery can shrink the pool without anyone deciding to (`c-ad-wording-and-targeting-exclude`).
Masculine-coded wording appeared in 97 percent of professional ads for male-dominated jobs, and in experiments
women rated the masculine-worded version of the same ad less appealing (4.16 against 4.50 of 6) and felt less
belonging, while perceived skill requirements did not change [77]. A gender-neutral STEM careers ad was shown to
fewer women than men because younger women are a more expensive audience and the delivery algorithm optimized
cost [66]; California treats targeted job advertisements as an automated-decision system [40], hence
`l-ad-delivery-audit`. Under 29 CFR 1625.4, terms such as "young", "college student" and "recent college
graduate" in an ad indicate an unlawful preference against workers forty and older unless a statutory exception
applies (`adea-advertising`) [78].

A degree or years-of-experience line is a quieter exclusion (`c-degree-requirement-shrinks-pool`). Roles
dropping degree requirements rose almost fourfold from 2014 to 2023, yet non-degree hiring in those roles rose
only 3.5 percentage points and the economy-wide effect was less than one hire in 700, because the screens did
not change; the non-degree hires that were made stayed longer [68] [58]. Years of experience predicts
performance at .07 [1]. The lever `l-skills-based-requirements` works only with a changed screen, which is the
skills-based-hiring controversy of chapter 8 [68] [44].

Which channel produces hires who stay is invisible without a record (`c-channel-mix-untested`), and the sources
differ: inside sources show higher tenure, less turnover and better performance than outside ones in the
meta-analysis the evidence summaries carry [62]. Until October 26, 2026, federal contractors also kept online
search and applicant records under the Internet Applicant rule, which goes with 41 CFR part 60-1 [87] [86]. The
Uniform Guidelines' selection-rate records remain and are the natural place for a source field, which is
`l-source-of-hire-tracking` [31].

Two national pools come with programs and duties attached. Veterans numbered 8.3 million in the labor force in
2025 at 3.5 percent unemployment, and 41.8 percent of employed Gulf War-era II men worked in management,
professional and related occupations [72]. Under the SkillBridge memorandum of understanding, transitioning
service members train with an industry partner during their last 180 days; an employer of 200 or fewer commits
to at least one participant a year and to a qualifying offer of immediate post-service employment for at least
75 percent of completers [74], while the service continues pay and benefits, as a law-firm summary states it
[59]; no study of its hiring outcomes was found [74]. A covered contractor also lists every opening, whether or
not contract-funded, with the state workforce agency job bank at least concurrently with any other recruiting
and asks for priority referral of protected veterans (`vevraa-job-listing`) [73]; the coverage threshold is
$200,000 under the September 2026 update [55]. Campus employers expected to hire 5.6 percent more Class of 2026
graduates [79]. The levers are `l-military-transition-pipeline` and `l-university-pipeline`.

The cleared pool is closed. "An individual may not obtain or initiate a security clearance on his or her own";
only a sponsoring agency can [80], so an already-cleared hire comes from someone another sponsor cleared
(`c-cleared-supply-scarce`). The last public count put about 4.2 million clearance holders on October 1, 2019,
1,346,765 of them contractors or other, and no later government count has been found [80]. A cleared-jobs
board's 2025 survey (13,177 usable responses) put average total compensation at $119,131, found 13 percent had
switched employers for pay in the past year, and priced a Secret clearance about $9,500 over Confidential and
Top Secret about $23,800 over Secret [89]. Sponsoring a new clearance moves the problem to the clear-and-start
stage, where the investigation clock decides [54] [56].

The contractor EEO framework resets on October 26, 2026 (`eeo-contractor-framework-2026`). After EO 14173
revoked Executive Order 11246 in January 2025 [55], the final rule of August 21, 2026 removes its implementing
regulations, 41 CFR parts 60-1, 60-2, 60-3, 60-4, 60-20, 60-40, 60-50 and 60-999 [86]. What remains is
statutory, as a law-firm alert reports it: Section 503 keeps a written plan at 50 employees and a $50,000
contract while losing its 7 percent goal and applicant self-identification, and VEVRAA keeps its plan and its
veteran applicant and hire reporting, both effective September 21, 2026 [55]. Title VII and the Uniform
Guidelines are unchanged [30] [31].

<!-- synthesis -->

Read together, the records order the causes of a thin pool. A posting whose title, pay figure or wording sends
it to the wrong queue is the cheapest thing to check [65] [77]; a credential line excludes capable people for
almost no validity [1] [68]; reach that depends on relocation depends on under three percent of job seekers
[234]; a referral network that is small or all alike gives the best hires and the same ones [60] [81]; an
unknown firm is judged on the posting and the recruiter alone [63] [64]; for cleared work the pool is fixed by
prior sponsorship [80]. Without a source field none of these can be told apart [62].

## 3. Pay in a rate-constrained firm: benchmarks, transparency, allowability, equity, counteroffers

<!-- figure: pay-transparency-effects -->

At the offer stage (`offer`) the firm names a number and the candidate decides, and the number has a ceiling the firm did not set. Under a time-and-materials or labor-hour contract each labor category carries a fixed hourly rate that already contains wages, overhead, general and administrative expense and profit, and it applies only to labor that meets the category's qualifications [98]; hours by an employee who does not meet them are not paid without the contracting officer's authorization [99]. The rate is fixed at award. The market is not: private-industry wages rose 3.1 percent and benefits 3.8 percent in the year to June 2026 [103].

<!-- synthesis -->

What that leaves for salary is arithmetic the records mark as synthesis. A government cost proposal builds a billing rate in layers, fringe on direct labor, overhead on labor and fringe, general and administrative expense on total cost input, and profit on top [206]; run backward, the category rate less those layers at the firm's own rates is the salary the rate can carry [98] [206], and `c-salary-above-rate` is the case where the market's number is higher. The lever `l-set-range-from-rate-and-market` checks that result against public benchmarks:
software developers averaged $148,100 a year in May 2025 [110];
cleared professionals averaged $119,131 in a job board's survey of 13,177 respondents,
with a Secret clearance worth about $9,500 over Confidential
and a Top Secret about $23,800 over Secret [89].

A rate that cannot move is the second constraint, `c-contract-rates-lag-market`. On GSA schedule contracts the economic price adjustment clause allows at most three increases per contract period, requested inside a window and thirty days apart, with contracting-officer approval for increases other than ten percent [117]. On service contracts the floors move on the Department of Labor's calendar: covered service employees receive the locally prevailing wage and benefits in the wage determination attached to the contract, plus a health-and-welfare rate of $5.55 an hour from July 2025 [101]. The lever `l-rate-relief-request` is a request inside the clause's window with the ECI series and CALC+ comparables attached [103] [111] [117].

What the candidate compares is wider than the salary line, the constraint `c-total-comp-gap-vs-large-employers`. Benefits were 30.0 percent of private-industry compensation cost in June 2026 and 31.5 percent for management, professional and related occupations [104]. The largest benefit has a price: the average family health premium was $26,993 in 2025, and 97 percent of firms with 200 or more workers offered coverage against 59 percent of firms with 10 to 199 [105]. The amenity evidence says what a firm can trade: in a discrete-choice experiment with applicants to a national call center, the average worker would give up about 8 percent of wages for the option to work from home and about 20 percent to avoid an employer-set, short-notice schedule [115]. Working conditions are compensation in their own right [94].

<!-- synthesis -->

The lever `l-total-rewards-statement` states the benefits and the arrangement in dollars and terms a candidate can compare [104] [105] [115]. Two limits follow. Across 71 studies, what the job and the organization are predicts attraction and choice [63], so noncash value does not offset a material base-pay gap; and a cleared, onsite job has no work-from-home option to price [115].

Transparency cuts both ways, and the pack carries the measured effects as the controversy `pay-transparency`; Figure 7 tabulates them. State laws protecting the right to discuss coworkers' pay lowered wages by about 2 percent, because an employer that would have to renegotiate with everyone credibly refuses to pay any one worker more, with the effect muted where pay was posted or collectively bargained [90]. State laws requiring a pay range in postings raised disclosure by about 30 percentage points and wages by 1.3 to 3.6 percent, with no effect on employment, posting volume or dispersion [97]. Cullen's taxonomy reconciles the two: cross-worker transparency shrinks coworker gaps but lowers average wages through harder bargaining, vertical transparency raises motivation, and cross-firm transparency moves workers toward higher-paying firms [91]. A posted range is the cross-firm kind.

Compression is the coworker side, and it lands on the people already doing the job, the constraint `c-new-hire-pay-compresses-incumbents`. When University of California employees were randomly informed of a public salary database, those paid below their unit's median reported lower satisfaction and were significantly more likely to look for a new job, while those above the median reported no gain [92]. In the one firm where the external premium was measured, the U.S. investment-banking arm of a financial-services firm from 2003 to 2009, external hires started about 18 percent higher than workers promoted into similar jobs, were outperformed by the promotees for two years and left more often [93].

<!-- synthesis -->

The response is asymmetric, so an offer above incumbents buys search below the new comparison point and nothing above it [92] [93]. The lever `l-internal-equity-review-before-offer` compares the offer with incumbents in substantially equal jobs, by content and not by title, and budgets the adjustments [112] [93]; it makes the retention cost visible and does not pay it [117] [98].

The law fixes what may be asked and how a difference is justified, the constraint `c-offer-process-exposure`. Under the Equal Pay Act men and women must receive equal pay for substantially equal work in the same establishment; a differential is lawful only on seniority, merit, quantity or quality of production, or a factor other than sex, the employer carries the burden, and no one's pay may be reduced to close a gap [112]. In the Ninth Circuit prior salary cannot justify a differential at all [116]. About twenty states bar all employers from asking about salary history, on a vendor's list [106]; the early bans raised the gender earnings ratio by about 1 percent, driven by recent job changers [96]. Posting laws add the range itself: thirteen states and the District of Columbia required a pay range in the posting on the January 2026 map [76], and Virginia, Maine and Connecticut joined during 2026, Virginia with a salary-history ban of its own, and Delaware follows in 2027 [223] [224].

Two more rules shape the offer letter. In a field experiment that randomly assigned nearly 2,500 job seekers to ads with and without a statement that wages were negotiable, men negotiated more than women when the ad was silent, and the gap disappeared or reversed when it said pay was negotiable [95]; the lever `l-post-and-state-negotiability` is that one sentence, and `l-salary-history-compliance` anchors the offer on the posted range instead of on prior pay [106] [116]. On cost-type contracts the cost principle at FAR 31.205-6 makes compensation allowable only when reasonable for the work and conforming to an established plan; a bonus is allowable only under an agreement or plan that existed before the services [100]. The benchmark cap, $671,000 for 2025 on an accounting firm's figure, applies to all employees on awards from June 24, 2014 [107] [100].

An accepted offer is not a start, the constraint `c-counteroffer-and-withdrawal` and the pattern `pass-then-decline`. The one field study of offer timing, archival data on 3,012 candidates at one large company, found earlier post-interview offers more likely to be accepted, with no difference in later performance or turnover [15]; speed is the one offer-stage lever with field evidence. What the surveys say about counteroffers is thin and dated: a staffing firm's 2018 survey of more than 5,500 U.S. hiring decision makers found that 58 percent of senior managers extend counteroffers and that employees who accept one stay a mean 1.7 years, and no peer-reviewed study of counteroffer outcomes was found [113]. Bonuses are common and unmeasured: in a compensation association's 2024 survey, 80 percent of organizations used sign-on bonuses and 12 percent evaluated whether their bonus programs work [118]; the lever `l-bonus-under-established-plan` carries them as practice under an allowability condition, not as evidence of effect [100] [118].

The last offer-stage constraint, `c-key-personnel-contingency`, is an offer that depends on an award the firm does not control. At GAO an offeror with actual knowledge that a proposed key person has become unavailable must notify the agency, after which the proposal is unacceptable unless discussions are reopened, and the duty binds protesters as well as awardees [108]. The Court of Federal Claims declined to impose that duty absent a solicitation provision, requiring only a reasonable belief at submission [109]. The gate `key-personnel-availability-rule` records the disagreement, and the lever `l-key-personnel-contingent-offer-protocol` reads the solicitation's notice terms, keeps a current letter of commitment and notifies promptly [109] [114] [108]. What the candidate does while waiting is the next chapter's subject.

## 4. From accepted offer to productive start: clearance, access, E-Verify, export control, background checks

<!-- figure: clearance-clock -->

The stage between acceptance and a productive first day (`clear-start`) runs on other people's clocks. Under the NISPOM rule at 32 CFR 117.10 a contractor may request eligibility only where access is essential to a classified contract, and requests "will not be used to establish a cache of cleared employees" [119]. The request also needs a facility clearance the firm cannot apply for itself: under 32 CFR 117.9 a government contracting activity or a cleared contractor sponsors it, and the senior management official, the facility security officer and the insider-threat official must hold eligibility [120]. That is the constraint `c-sponsorship-needs-contract-and-fcl`, which the levers `l-confirm-sponsorship-path-before-posting` and `l-facility-clearance-sponsorship` settle before a posting promises a date; neither the rule nor DCSA's roadmap states an end-to-end time [120] [146].

<!-- synthesis -->

Without the contract there is no request, and without the request no clock [119]. A requisition that reads "active clearance required" is often the only lawful shape for an opening with no DD 254 behind it [146], and it draws on a pool of about 1.35 million cleared contractor and other personnel on the 2019 count [80].

How long the clock runs depends on whose figure is read (`clearance-timeliness-figures`); Figure 3 sets them side by side. DCSA's industry cases in the third quarter of FY2026 averaged 197 days from initiation to adjudication for a Secret and 243 for a Top Secret, with adjudication now the longest phase [126]. Those are all-case averages on a basis adopted that quarter, not comparable with the earlier fastest-90-percent figures, 156 and 227 days in the first quarter [124] [126]. The government-wide quarterly report sets targets of 40 days for a moderate-risk and 75 for a high-risk position and reports a DCSA investigation inventory of about 121,000 and rising [54]. GAO found 63 percent of 305 clearance statistics it reviewed inaccurate or incomplete, mostly from a DCSA calculation that understated processing time from 2020 to 2024 [56].

Against that clock the median time to fill a role in 2025 was about 45 days [43], and a candidate investigated before employment must begin within 45 days of the grant [119]; that is `c-clearance-clock-exceeds-vacancy-window`. Temporary eligibility under SEAD 8 [121] can bridge the gap where no adverse information is found, but it is not valid for RD, COMSEC or NATO information until the final grant [119], and no public figure says how often or how fast a contractor applicant receives an interim [127]. Adjudication applies the thirteen guidelines of SEAD 4 under the whole-person concept [123]; in a vendor's count of 624 published DOHA appeals for 2024, financial issues outnumbered all other issues combined [151].

<!-- synthesis -->

The only phase the firm influences is initiation, about three weeks of the total; the investigation and the adjudication belong to DCSA and the adjudicative facility [126] [119]. `l-two-track-hiring-plan` sets each opening's start date from the clock it sits on, five business days for a reciprocity decision against 197 to 243 days for a new industry case [122] [126], and `l-temporary-eligibility-request` submits the questionnaire and fingerprints as early as the commitment allows [119] [146].

Eligibility is not access, the constraint `c-eligibility-is-not-access`. A favorable determination lets the firm grant access to classified information; it does not put the hire at a desk. A Common Access Card needs a DoD government sponsor, a favorable FBI fingerprint check and a submitted investigation [138], and an interim card is not transferable or reciprocally accepted [137]. A cyber work role has a day-one rule: "Contracted support personnel must meet foundational qualification requirements upon commencement of cyberspace work" [139]. At DHS the interval from favorable determination to onboarding ran 41 days in FY2026's third quarter, up from 35 [125].

A clearance held elsewhere moves by reciprocity under SEAD 7, which covers contractors. An agency accepts an investigation and adjudication by an authorized agency unless one of seven exceptions applies, reciprocity cannot be denied for want of a polygraph, and the determination "shall be made within five business days of receipt" [122]. The rule is not a measured outcome: GAO found the government's reciprocity data inconsistent and incomplete, 28 of 31 surveyed agencies said their systems at times lacked the information a decision needed, and contractors reported hearing nothing while transfers were delayed [145]. The lever `l-reciprocity-first-recruiting` collects the level, the investigation date and whether the grant was final before the offer, at the cost of a narrower pool [122] [80].

The wait has a measured cost, the constraint `c-long-interval-loses-accepted-hires`. Speer and colleagues examined 157,154 accepted entry-level offers at one large telecommunications company between May 2019 and June 2025: 4.1 percent of hires never showed up, and each additional week between acceptance and the start date raised the odds of a no-show by 22 percent (OR 1.22) [144]. A second study of 434 U.S. adults found that an employee referral (OR 0.42) and good communication from the company (OR 0.56) lowered the odds [144]. The population is entry-level and customer-facing, so the rates do not transfer to cleared technical hiring; only the direction does [144]. The lever `l-offer-to-start-interval-management` is the study's recommendation, a shorter interval, a named contact and a pre-start check-in [144]; the pattern `accept-cannot-start` is what the stage looks like when it fails.

<!-- synthesis -->

What the firm may do with the hire before access is bounded. It may not sponsor the clearance ahead of a contract need to shorten the wait [119]. A time-and-materials or labor-hour contract pays only for qualified labor performed on the contract [98] [99], and labor that cannot be charged to a contract is an indirect cost, pooled into the firm's rates [148]. Whether an uncleared hire may do unclassified contract work depends on the contract's security terms [146]. That is `c-no-chargeable-work-before-access`, and the lever `l-bridge-work-plan` decides who pays for the wait before the offer is made [148]; no study of bridge-work practice was found [144].

The start carries statutory clocks of its own, the constraint `c-start-date-compliance-clocks`. Section 2 of Form I-9 must be completed within three business days of the date employment begins, and the employee chooses which acceptable documents to present [130]. The E-Verify clause at FAR 52.222-54 attaches to contracts over $150,000 with work in the United States [129], and a contractor initiates verification of each new hire within three business days of the hire date [128]. A background report under the Fair Credit Reporting Act requires a written, stand-alone disclosure and written permission before the report is obtained, and a copy of the report with the summary of rights before any adverse action [136]. Criminal-history questions wait until after a conditional offer under the Fair Chance Act, with exceptions for positions requiring access to classified information [48].

<!-- synthesis -->

Drug testing is no longer a federal contract requirement. The DFARS clause that required contractor drug-testing programs on sensitive work, 252.223-7004, is reserved as of the May 7, 2026 DFARS change [134], and the FAR's drug-free workplace program that remains requires a policy statement, an awareness program and conviction reporting, with no testing [135]. Testing is therefore firm policy and state law unless a customer requires it (`drug-free-workplace`) [134] [135]. The lever `l-start-date-compliance-sequence` orders the checks by their legal trigger, because a step taken out of order is itself a violation [136] [48]. Title VII's national-security exception makes it lawful to refuse a person who has not met a national-security requirement imposed on the position [140], but not to impose a citizenship restriction the law does not require [131].

A foreign national is the last constraint, `c-foreign-national-hire-blocked`. Under ITAR, releasing technical data to a foreign person in the United States is itself an export [132], and a U.S. person is a lawful permanent resident or a protected individual under the anti-discrimination statute, which reaches citizens, nationals, refugees and asylees [133]. The Justice Department's fact sheet tells employers to assess only controlled positions, apart from the Form I-9, never to say the export rules require a citizenship, and to seek authorization before a non-U.S. person does the work [131]; settlements for citizens-only postings ran from $365,000
to $500,000 per employer in 2023 [49].
The $100,000 payment on new H-1B entries was extended twelve months from September 21, 2026 [142], but the District of Massachusetts vacated the implementing policy on June 8, 2026, the First Circuit denied a stay on July 24, 2026, and the payment is uncollected pending appeal [143], while a District of Columbia court upheld the proclamation [226] [225].
A proposed $103,265 fee on cap-subject H-1B petitions is not final [141] (`ch-017`, `ch-034`, `ch-018`).

The start-date clocks that bind have different owners: the 45-day start after a pre-employment grant [119], the five-business-day reciprocity decision [122], the three business days for the I-9's Section 2 and for E-Verify [130] [128], the FCRA's notices [136], the day-one cyber qualification [139], and the credential, the read-in and the customer's onboarding interval [138] [125]; the lever `l-pre-start-access-checklist` lists each with its owner and date [138] [122] [139]. After the start comes continuous vetting: industry had about 984,000 enrollments in the third quarter of FY2026 [126], enrollment stood at 78 percent of the covered population (`ind-tw2-continuous-vetting-fy26q3`), and full enrollment is planned for September 2028 [54].

## 5. Onboarding, mentoring and who pays for training

The `onboard` stage is the first ninety days. The 2025 meta-analysis of newcomer socialization, 183 samples of people in their first year, finds that a firm's structured tactics correlate .24 to .40 with role clarity, task mastery and social acceptance, and that the social tactics, an experienced insider as a guide and affirmation of what the hire already brings, are the strongest at .29 to .47 [152]. Social acceptance then carries the outcomes: .35 with performance, −.27 with intentions to leave and −.12 with leaving itself [152]. The links from adjustment to actual turnover are small [152].

Two single-site studies agree. At one large financial-services organization with no standardized orientation, 24.8 percent of 222 newcomers left voluntarily within a year; experienced insiders as role models roughly halved the odds (OR 0.44), as did affirming who the newcomer already was (OR 0.52) [155]. At one research university, fourteen weekly surveys of new hires found supervisor and coworker support falling over the first 14 weeks and a supervisor who undermined the newcomer tripling the hazard of leaving (HR 3.01) [156]. Neither is a cleared technical workforce; the direction is what the pack carries [155].

<!-- synthesis -->

That is the first onboard constraint, `c-newcomer-adjustment-left-to-chance`, and the first cause the pattern `start-then-struggle` points at [152]. Adjustment has two sources, the firm's tactics and the newcomer's proactivity [152] [153], and a small firm with no program leaves the second to carry the whole load (synthesis). The lever `l-structured-onboarding-program` writes the first week, month and quarter down, names an insider, and holds supervisor contact steady [152] [156]. It does not make an unqualified hire meet a labor category [99], and the social tactics cost senior hours [152].

The second constraint is the ramp, measured once cleanly. At one autoglass company whose installers' output is counted per worker, output was 53 percent higher after one year than at hire, the workers who stayed learned fastest, and pay profiles were much flatter than output profiles for about the first 18 months [157]. The job is manual and the slope does not transfer to an engineer; no public benchmark for a technical professional's ramp was found, so `c-ramp-to-productivity-exceeds-plan` carries the shape and the pay–output gap and nothing more [157].

<!-- synthesis -->

The ramp collides with the contract. Under a time-and-materials contract the hourly rate is the rate for labor meeting the qualifications of a specified labor category, and it already contains wages, overhead and profit [98]; the payment clause pays for hours on such tasks only to the extent they are performed by employees who meet the qualifications, unless the contracting officer authorizes otherwise [99]. The rate assumes the finished worker from the first hour while the firm pays for the unfinished one, and the gap is margin or indirect cost (synthesis from [98] [157] [148]). `l-readiness-milestones-plan` makes the ramp visible with dated milestones that say what the person can do and bill at each [157].

The third onboard constraint, `c-customer-rejects-junior-staff`, is the same rule from the customer's side, enforced after the fact. In 2018 the Department of Defense Inspector General examined 12 OASIS task orders and found DoD improperly charged for 101 of 112 contractor employees who did not meet their labor-category requirements, about $35 million in potential improper payments [173]. A cyber hire must also meet DoD Manual 8140.03's foundational qualification requirements on the day the work starts [139]. The lever `l-supervised-junior-work-package` proposes a junior category with a supervised scope, billed at the category the person meets [99] [173]; it cannot create a category the contract lacks [98].

The `develop` stage is where the firm spends money and senior time making a hire worth more. The standard theory says workers pay for general training because they can take it anywhere; Acemoglu and Pischke answer that firms pay for it because the employer who trained someone knows what they can do better than rival employers do, an information advantage that amounts to ex post monopsony power [161]. On British industry data from 1983 to 1996, a one percentage point rise in training was associated with about 0.6 percent more value added per hour and about 0.3 percent higher wages, so the firm holds the difference while the worker stays [159]. On German establishment data, between 4 and 9 percent of total compensation arrives as learning from more knowledgeable coworkers [158].

<!-- synthesis -->

The same arithmetic says who will come for the trained person. General skills raise the outside value as fast as the inside value, and the firm's hold is its information advantage [161] and whatever pay progression and retention terms it still has (synthesis). At one large establishment tuition reimbursement raised retention, but 80 percent of the effect came through sorting, who chose to join a firm that offered it [160]. At the unit level, firm-specific training is the practice most strongly tied to lower turnover rates (−.40 across 82 studies) and general training is not significant [181]. The cleared market is already pricing the capability: 13 percent of respondents to the 2025 cleared-compensation survey had switched employers for pay in the past year [89]. That is `c-training-investment-walks-out`, and it feeds the retain stage's poaching constraint (synthesis).

The instruments once used to hold the investment are narrowing, which is the gate `training-repayment-and-stay-or-pay`. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau's 2023 spotlight treats training repayment provisions as employer-driven debt: nearly 10 percent of American workers surveyed in 2020 were covered by one, and the research the Bureau cites puts a training contract's effect at around 15 percent less quitting [166].

California's AB 692 makes it unlawful, for contracts entered after January 1, 2026, to require repayment of training, education, relocation, signing or retention amounts for leaving before a date, with exceptions for a transferable credential under a separate, prorated, at-cost agreement and for approved apprenticeships, no repayment on a termination except for misconduct, and a $5,000 penalty or actual damages [167].

Colorado permits repayment only for training distinct from normal on-the-job training, at reasonable cost declining proportionally over two years, with a $5,000 per-worker penalty; the NLRB general counsel's memoranda against stay-or-pay terms were rescinded; and the FTC rule that would have treated some such provisions as noncompetes never took effect [168]. On September 5, 2025 the Commission acceded to the district court's vacatur of that rule, leaving noncompetes to state law (`noncompete-agreements-federal-status`) [169].

The state picture kept moving through 2026. New York's Trapped at Work Act makes employment promissory notes unenforceable from February 13, 2027, with fines of $1,000 to $5,000 per violation [212] [211]. Washington's ESHB 1155 goes further from June 30, 2027: every noncompetition covenant becomes void regardless of when it was signed, repayment and forfeiture terms tied to working in one's field count as noncompetes, and the only repayment agreement left standing is one for educational expenses that expires within 18 months of the start date [210]. The lever `l-training-with-lawful-retention-terms` is the shape that fits all of them: the training offered openly in the posting [160], and any repayment term as a separate agreement for a transferable credential, at actual cost, prorated, without interest and never enforced on a termination without misconduct [167] [168].

Three more gates apply. Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, a non-exempt employee's training time counts as hours worked unless it is outside normal hours, voluntary, not job related and free of other work (`flsa-training-time`) [170]. Under FAR 31.205-44, training related to the field in which the employee works or may reasonably be expected to work is an allowable cost, and training for other than bona fide employees is not, the line that bounds a developmental pipeline (`far-31-205-44-training-costs`) [175]; the cost sits in the indirect pools and comes back through the rates [148]. Under section 127 an employer may provide up to $5,250 per employee per year of educational assistance excluded from wages, under a separate written plan employees have been told about; the limit is indexed for taxable years after 2026 (`section-127-educational-assistance`) [174].

<!-- synthesis -->

`c-mentoring-capacity-is-senior-billable-time` puts the senior-time arithmetic plainly: the insider beside the newcomer is the strongest onboarding tactic [152] and learning from coworkers is 4 to 9 percent of what a job pays [158], but the senior's hours are contract hours [99], so mentoring outside the statement of work is indirect cost [148] paid from margin (synthesis). The protégé's measurable gains are small: the 2004 meta-analysis of workplace mentoring found small effects on compensation and promotions [164], and the 2008 meta-analysis across settings found a generally small effect size [165]. `l-protected-mentoring-time` budgets the hours in the indirect rates before the hire and names one senior per trainee [175] [148]; no study of how many trainees a senior can carry was found (synthesis).

<!-- synthesis -->

`c-training-content-unlike-the-work` is the transfer problem. The transfer-of-training review finds the most consistent support for realistic training environments, a supervisor who expects the skill to be used (ρ .31, against .14 for peer support), a transfer climate (.37), motivation to transfer (.43) and an early opportunity to perform [162]. `l-representative-task-training` builds the course from sanitized tasks that look like the work, has the supervisor say what will change, and schedules the first real use within weeks [162]; on a classified program a sanitized task is the ceiling (synthesis).

<!-- synthesis -->

The last two constraints are about placement and measurement. `c-development-without-placement` is bounded by rules written elsewhere: training for non-employees is unallowable [175], a clearance may be requested only for an employee a classified contract needs [119], and a SkillBridge partner owes qualifying offers to at least 75 percent of completers [74]; `l-tie-development-to-named-demand` writes the target contract, category and date into the plan before the hire [175]. `l-registered-apprenticeship` is the formal route: the program had about 940,000 participants in FY2024, computer occupations third at 7 percent, and GAO heard that registration and reporting were especially challenging for smaller employers and that cost deterred participation [172]. The federal cost-benefit study measured completers' career earnings gains of $240,037 and nothing on the employer's side [171], which is `c-development-return-unmeasured`: the economics gives the terms [159] [160], and nothing tells a small firm what its own cohort returned. `l-cohort-return-tracking` records training cost, mentoring hours, billed hours and status at twelve and twenty-four months for each trainee and judges the cohort over two years (synthesis). The controversy `who-pays-for-training` carries the positions.

<!-- section: 6-why-people-stay-and-why-they-leave -->
## 6. Why people stay and why they leave: the turnover evidence and what a small firm can change

<!-- figure: turnover-predictors -->

The `retain` stage has something the earlier stages lack, a ranked list, and the pattern `productive-employees-leave` is ordered by it [178]. Figure 2 draws it from the 2018 meta-analysis of voluntary turnover, 316 articles and 57 predictors, mostly U.S. samples [178]. Wanting to leave, the withdrawal cognitions, predicts actual leaving at a corrected correlation of .56 and job search at .40; then commitment (−.29), fit (−.29), satisfaction (−.28) and embeddedness (−.26); then leadership (−.24) and the alternatives a person perceives (.23); stress and exhaustion at .21, work–life conflict at .19, role ambiguity and role conflict at .15 each; and pay at −.17, with hours of workload at −.10 and performance at −.08 [178].

Pay moves people through comparison. At a large U.S. retailer the quit response to own pay mostly reflected comparisons with higher-paid peers, and separations were fairly insensitive when raises were similar across peers [182]. University employees randomly informed of a public salary database behaved the same way: those below their unit's median became more likely to look for a new job, and those above gained nothing [92]. Each market-rate hire resets the comparison, since external hires at one financial-services firm started about 18 percent above the workers promoted into similar jobs and were outperformed by them for two years [93], and 13 percent of surveyed cleared professionals switched employers for pay in a year [89]. That is `c-pay-lags-capability-and-peers`, and the room to answer is bounded by a labor-category rate that already contains the wage, the indirect cost and the profit [98].

The lever `l-pay-review-against-market-and-peers` follows that evidence: review pay when capability changes, compare within the firm first, and keep raises similar across people doing substantially equal work unless the difference can be explained [182]. The Equal Pay Act is the floor, since substantially equal work in the same establishment must be paid equally unless one of its four defenses applies, and no employee's pay may be reduced to close a gap [112].

A small firm's second problem is the ladder, `c-few-steps-up-in-a-small-firm`. Internal mobility is among the practices most strongly associated with lower unit-level turnover, ρ −.25 across 82 studies [181], and a firm with one senior per specialty has little of it to give. The step it does have is usually management. Across sales workers at 214 firms, firms promoted on current sales performance even when other traits better predicted managerial success [184]; a doubling of a manager's pre-promotion sales corresponded to a 7.5 percent decline in the manager's value added, and workers with more teamwork experience made better managers without being promoted more often [185]. `l-technical-ladder-and-lateral-moves` offers scope and pay steps that are not the management title [184] [185]; the evidence is from sales organizations [184].

<!-- synthesis -->

The third retain constraint, `c-disconnection-from-the-firm-and-the-work`, is the condition of the whole pack: an employee at a customer site works under the customer's manager, for the customer's goals, with the firm present on payday (synthesis). The predictors that cluster next after wanting to leave and looking are commitment, fit, satisfaction, embeddedness and leadership, all between −.24 and −.29 [178]; across 7,939 business units, engagement relates to turnover, productivity, profit and customer satisfaction [183]. `l-manager-check-ins-and-stay-conversations` keeps a manager of the firm's own in regular contact with each person at the site [178]; stay interviews and recognition programs as such have no evidence base found (synthesis). Embeddedness predicts quitting at −.26 [178], and at one firm on-the-job embeddedness predicted lower turnover (OR 0.42) [155]; the practices suggested to build it are untested [192] (`l-embeddedness-building`).

<!-- synthesis -->

What an overloaded team does to turnover is where the records corrected the author's own hypothesis. Hours of workload as such barely register, ρ −.10, while stress and exhaustion predict quitting at .21, work–life conflict at .19, and role ambiguity and role conflict at .15 each [178]; at the unit level, staffing levels were not significantly related to turnover rates [181]. On a time-and-materials contract a vacancy is unbilled hours [99], and the firm's instinct is to cover them with the people who remain; what follows is not more quitting from more hours but more quitting from the strain and the conflict that the covering creates, which is `c-vacancy-overload-cascade` [178] (synthesis). `l-capacity-signalling-and-scope-negotiation` puts the gap in front of the customer with its date and asks for a priority call, reduced scope or temporary support [188]; the customer may decline (synthesis).

An onsite, fixed-schedule job competes against arrangements it cannot offer. Applicants to a national call center would give up about 8 percent of wages for the option to work from home and about 20 percent to avoid an employer-set, short-notice schedule [115]; teachers in one district who commuted 45 minutes or more were 10 percentage points more likely to transfer than colleagues at the same school commuting five minutes [194]. Classified work happens where the classified information is [119], and `c-onsite-schedule-and-commute` is the result. `l-approved-schedule-flexibility` asks the contracting officer in writing for whatever the contract allows and states the limits accurately in the posting, since perceived honesty is the mechanism by which realistic previews lower turnover [13].

<!-- synthesis -->

The job ends when the contract does, and the person knows it. A visible recompete supplies the strongest predictor, wanting to leave, and the alternatives a person sees [178]. Executive Order 14055 had given qualified employees on a federal service contract a right of first refusal with the successor contractor; EO 14148 revoked it on January 20, 2025, the Department of Labor removed 29 CFR part 9 effective December 22, 2025, and the FAR Council had never issued the implementing clause, so the right never bound a contractor (`service-contract-transition-rules`) [186]. Under the WARN Act an employer with 100 or more employees owes 60 calendar days' written notice before a plant closing or mass layoff affecting 50 or more employees at a single site (`warn-act`) [191]; most small firms are below that threshold (synthesis), though fifteen states are reported to have mini-WARN laws [213], Washington's at 50 employees [215]. `c-contract-discontinuity-fear` has one lever, `l-honest-pipeline-communication`: the option years, recompetes and protests as facts with dates, the redeployment options that exist, and no implied right of first refusal [186] [13].

<!-- synthesis -->

The prime, the customer or a competitor hires the person the firm trained. Under the January 2025 DOJ and FTC guidelines, agreements between competing employers not to recruit, solicit or hire each other's workers are illegal even without actual harm and may be prosecuted as felonies, and a no-hire or no-solicit term gets an effects analysis only when it is subordinate and collateral to a legitimate collaboration and reasonably necessary to it (`no-poach-and-non-solicitation`) [187]. Restrictions on the person are a state matter after the federal noncompete rule's vacatur [169]. With restrictions on the hiring party criminal and restrictions on the person state-limited, the hold has to be in the job, embeddedness at −.26 [178] and firm-specific training at −.40 with unit turnover [181], the conclusion `c-poaching-by-prime-or-customer` records (synthesis). `l-lawful-non-solicitation-terms` strikes bare no-hire terms from teaming agreements and scopes any non-solicit to the collaboration and its duration [187].

<!-- synthesis -->

The `handover` stage is where a departure meets the contract and the security rules. Where a contract names key personnel, the HHS clause carried as the example treats them as essential to performance: no diversion, replacement or announced change without the contracting officer's written consent, at least 30 days' notice before a voluntary move with a replacement whose skills, experience and credentials meet or exceed the requirements, and the maximum practicable notice where a person leaves on less than 30 days (`key-personnel-substitution-clause`) [188]. Before award, an offeror that knows a proposed key person has become unavailable must notify the agency [108]. For a cleared employee, 32 CFR 117.8 requires the contractor to report the termination and to keep reporting adverse information from verified sources, a duty the termination does not negate (`nispom-reporting-and-continuous-vetting`) [189], and the employee carries continuing self-reporting duties under SEAD 3 [190]. `l-departure-protocol` writes those clocks down for the day notice is given, and `c-departure-triggers-contract-duties` records why: the contract treats the named person as part of the deliverable and the security rules treat them as a reportable event, so the firm's obligations run to the customer and the agency before they run to the backfill (synthesis from [188] [189]).

<!-- synthesis -->

The cost of the departure is largest where the knowledge is concentrated, which is `c-knowledge-concentrated-in-few-people`. In German records of 34,000 unexpected worker deaths, a death raised the remaining workers' wages and retention, coworkers in other occupations lost when a specialized worker died, and replacement costs were larger in thin labor markets and for specialized skills [193]. A small technical firm with one person per specialty is that case by construction (synthesis). `l-cross-training-and-documented-handoffs` trains a second holder for every single-holder task and names a successor who meets the labor category [188]; firm-specific training is the practice most strongly tied to a unit staying together [181], and succession metrics are part of the human-capital reporting standard [46]. An internal move is the other way to keep someone, and `c-internal-move-opens-another-vacancy` names its price: internal mobility keeps people [181], and the move leaves a vacancy with the full clock, 197 to 243 days where a clearance is needed [126], while a named person's move needs the customer's consent first [188].

<!-- synthesis -->

Why the cost of turnover is disputed is the controversy `cost-of-turnover`. Practitioner literature puts the total cost of replacing an employee at 90 to 200 percent of salary, and the specimen the pack carries names no study [195]. The one systematic look at the measured case studies found a median of about 21 percent of annual salary across 27 case studies, 16 percent for jobs paying $30,000 or less and up to 213 percent at the top; only 2 of 11 papers counted indirect costs [180]. Across 82 studies, turnover rates correlate about −.22 with customer satisfaction and with production efficiency and −.15 with profit margin, the same for voluntary and total turnover [181]. The practitioner review that ranked the predictors for managers says the practice literature carries misconceptions [179]. The records' own conclusion is marked as such: plan with the measured fifth for ordinary replacements, count the indirect costs the case studies left out, and treat the specialist in a thin market as the exception the multiples were invented for, where the cost is the contract's capacity, the customer's satisfaction and the pay of everyone adjacent [180] [181] [193] (synthesis). For scale, quits ran 1.9 percent a month economy-wide in August 2026 [57].

## 7. Planning the workforce: demand, vacancy cost, priority, key personnel and labor categories

<!-- figure: cost-of-a-hire -->

The plan stage `plan` comes before the posting: the firm decides whether an opening exists, what it is, what it can
pay and what it will cost. Most of what settles those questions sits outside the firm. The customer and the
contract decide whether the opening is funded, which labor category it bills and who approves the person [119] [99]
[197]; the market sets the price [110] [103]; the law sets the records and the partner limits [200] [198]. Figure 8
collects the cost and time figures the stage runs on.

<!-- synthesis -->
What is left to the firm is the forecast, the scorecard, the budget, the exposure cap and the owner; the planning
principles [205] and the recruiting benchmarks [196] give each its shape without evidence that any of them changes
an outcome.

An opening is real when the rules say it is. A contractor requests eligibility only where access is essential to a
classified contract, and the requests "will not be used to establish a cache of cleared employees" [119]; a
candidate investigated before employment needs a written commitment and must start within 45 days of the grant
[119]. A person named in a proposal is a hire contingent on award, and an offeror that knows a proposed key person
has become unavailable must tell the agency [108]. GAO's ASRC decision shows the cost: a proposed key person had
committed to the protester and said so, her résumé went in anyway, the agency credited a strength on which an 18.5
percent price premium rested, and GAO sustained the protest [203]. Recruiting spend before funding is indirect cost
[148], and the wait between acceptance and start is where hires are lost: among 157,154 accepted entry-level offers
at one telecommunications employer, 4.1 percent never showed, and each extra week raised the odds of a no-show (OR
1.22) [144]. The constraint `c-opening-not-yet-real`, the gate `proposal-staffing-representations` and the lever
`l-confirm-the-opening` apply one test: contract, category, funding line, decision authority and start date,
written down before any spend [119] [108] [203].

<!-- synthesis -->
On a customer-governed requisition "qualified" has three definitions, and
`c-firm-and-customer-define-qualified-differently` holds them side by side: one enforceable, one valid, one
decisive. The contract's definition is the labor category's education and experience line; under a
time-and-materials or labor-hour contract, hours worked by people who do not meet it are not paid without the
contracting officer's authorization [99]. An audit of twelve OASIS task orders found DoD improperly charged for 101
of 112 employees who did not meet their category, 1,219 of 1,287 qualifications never validated by résumé review,
and about $35 million in potential improper payments [173] (`ind-oasis-improper-payments-2018`). The evidence's
definition is a structured, scored assessment of the work: structured interviews predict performance at .42 and
work samples at .33, against .19 for unstructured interviews and .07 for years of experience [1], and combining the
same information by formula reaches .44 where judgment reaches .28 [7]. The customer's definition is whatever its
reviewer applies, within FAR 37.104 (`far-37-104-personal-services`): without statutory authority an agency may not
award a contract characterized by an employer-employee relationship with the contractor's personnel, which arises
from relatively continuous supervision and control; giving orders for a specific result, with the right to reject
the product, does not by itself create it [197]. The customer may set the qualifications the contract requires and
accept or reject the product, including by approving named staff; one that selects, directs and rates the firm's
individual employees moves toward the relationship the rule forbids [197].

The pattern `customer-rejects` orders its causes from the records: the three definitions; a preference inherited
from the customer or the last incumbent and written into the posting as a requirement nobody owns
(`c-preferences-written-as-requirements`); a requisition that describes two jobs so the candidate fits one
(`c-requisition-blends-several-jobs`); a review with no clock, or a junior in a senior slot [99] [1] [197] [68]
[65] [188] [173]. Roles that dropped degree requirements rose almost fourfold from 2014 to 2023 while non-degree
hiring in them rose only 3.5 percentage points, because the screens did not change [68] [58], and a requirement
with adverse impact must be job-related and consistent with business necessity [30] [32]. The lever
`l-requisition-scorecard-with-customer` labels each requirement as contractual, customer-required in writing or
preferred, names who can waive it, and reviews an example profile with the customer before posting [99] [197]; no
study of shared scorecards or of rejection feedback was found, and restarting after a requirement change still
loses engaged candidates [16].

Where the customer approves named staff, the approval has no clock and the candidate does
(`c-customer-approval-cycle`): the key-personnel clause carried as the example requires 30 days' notice and an
equal-or-better substitute from the contractor and sets no period for the government's answer [188]. At one large
company, earlier post-interview offers were accepted more often, with no difference in later performance or
turnover [15]; among 3,550 police applicants, those who withdrew differed in how they perceived the organization
and the process [16]. The lever `l-approval-milestones-and-escalation` has the review steps, their days and an
escalation contact agreed before the first résumé goes, and the candidate told the calendar [188] [144]. Whether
one accountable owner shortens the clocks is unstudied; `l-one-requisition-owner` rests on the delays it is meant
to prevent, in small organizations running a median of six requisitions per recruiter [196]
(`ind-shrm-requisitions-per-recruiter-2025`).

<!-- synthesis -->
Whether a role can be staffed at all is arithmetic (`c-role-structurally-unprofitable`). A government cost proposal
builds a billing rate in layers, fringe on labor, overhead on direct labor and fringe, general and administrative
expense on total cost input, and profit as a rate [206]. Under a time-and-materials contract each labor category's
hourly rate already contains wages, overhead, G&A and profit, and it was fixed at award [98]; a schedule rate rises
at most three times a period, inside a window [117]. Market pay is public, software developers at a median of
$65.38 an hour and a mean of
$148,100 a year in May 2025 [110], and wages rose 3.1 percent in the year to June 2026
[103]. The lever `l-wrap-rate-feasibility-check` loads the market salary with those layers in that order and
compares it with the category rate or the public schedule ceiling [206] [98] [111]; where it does not fit, the role
loses money at any hire, and redistributing the work, re-teaming at mixed categories or seeking adjustment inside
the clause's window are each moves the contract may bar [117].

<!-- synthesis -->
A hire costs three things and only the first appears on an invoice (`c-requisition-economics-unseen`). The
acquisition spend is benchmarked: in a 2025 survey of 2,371 SHRM members the median cost per hire was $1,200 for
nonexecutive roles and $10,625 for executive roles, with agency fees among the components [196]
(`ind-shrm-cost-per-hire-2025`). The vacancy is harder to see: the median cost of replacing a worker was about 21
percent of annual salary across 27 case studies excluding executives and physicians, 16 percent for jobs paying
$30,000 or less and up to 213 percent at the top, with only 2 of 11 papers counting indirect costs [180]
(`ind-turnover-cost-share-of-salary`); unbilled hours are revenue the contract never pays [99]; across 82 studies,
unit turnover correlated about −.22 with customer satisfaction and production efficiency and −.15 with profit
margin [181] (`ind-collective-turnover-performance-2013`). The cash is a timing gap
(`prompt-payment-and-accelerated-payment`): payment is due 30 days after receipt of a proper invoice [202],
agencies pay small business contractors with a goal of 15 days [201], and payroll before the first invoice sits in
the indirect pools [148]. The levers `l-all-in-acquisition-budget`, `l-vacancy-cost-estimate` and
`l-hiring-ahead-exposure-cap` set a budget per role against the vacancy's cost, rank openings by that cost and by
feasibility, and cap the payroll carried before billing [196] [180] [202]; no primary on agency fee levels was
found.

Recruiting starts when the vacancy opens, and every vacancy is treated alike (`c-no-forecast-until-urgent`). The
government's planning principles ask an organization to determine the critical skills it will need, tailor
strategies to the gaps, build the capability and monitor progress [205], which is guidance, not evidence of effect.
A firm that begins at the vacancy faces a median fill of about 45 days [43] (`ind-shrm-time-to-fill-2025`) or an
industry clearance clock of 197 days for a Secret and 243 for a Top Secret in the third quarter of FY2026 [126]. In
September 2026, 87 percent of small-business owners hiring or trying to hire reported few or no qualified
applicants [71]. The lever `l-rolling-demand-forecast` keeps a dated list of expected openings by category,
contract and probability [205].

Partner support is capped, consented and competitively constrained (`c-partner-support-limits`). On a set-aside or
sole-source services contract the prime will not pay more than 50 percent of the amount paid by the government to
subcontractors that are not similarly situated, measured at the end of each period [198]
(`limitations-on-subcontracting`). Without an approved purchasing system the contractor needs the contracting
officer's advance consent for any cost-reimbursement, time-and-materials or labor-hour subcontract, with the
subcontractor named and priced, and consent does not relieve the contractor of responsibility [199]
(`consent-to-subcontract`). Agreements between competing employers not to recruit or hire each other's workers are
illegal even without actual harm and may be prosecuted as felonies; a non-solicit term is judged on its effects
only where it is collateral to a legitimate collaboration and reasonably necessary to it [187]
(`no-poach-and-non-solicitation`). The lever `l-customer-approved-partner-support` works inside those three lines,
at the cost of margin, control and the customer's approval [198] [199] [187].

Staffing decisions run on anecdote where the records are not kept (`c-decisions-on-anecdote`). Among 959 HR
professionals surveyed, beliefs diverged from the research most on selection [204]. The law already requires most
of the record. Under 29 CFR 1602.14, application forms and records of hiring, promotion, transfer, termination, pay
and selection for training are kept one year from the record or the personnel action, and all relevant records
until final disposition once a charge is filed [200]; the Uniform Guidelines require records by race, sex and
ethnic group sufficient to compute adverse impact [31]; California's automated-decision rules require four years
[40]; the contractor-specific record duties under the EO 11246 regulations go on October 26, 2026 [86]. The lever
`l-pipeline-record-and-evidence-review` keeps one record per requisition of where each candidate stopped and why,
inside the gate `applicant-record-retention`, and reviews stage losses and source yields against the evidence [200]
[31] [204].

## 8. The controversies

The first dispute, `cognitive-ability-validity`, asks whether general mental ability is the best single predictor
of job performance or a mid-table one with the largest adverse-impact exposure. Schmidt and Hunter put GMA at .51
and named GMA with an integrity test (R = .65), a work sample (.63) or a structured interview (.63) as the best
combinations [4]. Sackett, Zhang, Berry and Lievens argued in 2022 that earlier meta-analyses had overcorrected
concurrent studies for range restriction; uncorrected, cognitive ability falls to .31, structured interviews lead
at .42, and cognitive ability carries a Black–White d of .79 [1] [3]. Oh, Le and Roth replied that the conditions
the revision treats as negligible are plausible, so the no-correction recommendation lacks support [2].
Practitioner summaries call it a course correction [52] [53].

<!-- synthesis -->
Both sides agree on the ordering that matters to a small firm: a structured interview, a job-knowledge test and a
work sample each predict well, and a cognitive test adds the largest group difference of any common predictor [1]
[4]. The dispute is about one coefficient's size, not whether structure beats judgment; a firm that scores a
structured interview and a work sample mechanically and monitors selection rates satisfies both.

The second, `skills-based-hiring`, asks whether the removal of degree requirements announced at scale from 2017
changed who was hired. The Burning Glass Institute and Harvard Business School found roles dropping degree
requirements rose almost fourfold from 2014 to 2023 while non-degree hiring in them rose only 3.5 percentage
points, an economy-wide effect of less than one hire in 700 [68] [58]. The Hidden Workers report blamed the
filters: 88 percent of surveyed employers said their systems rejected qualified high-skilled candidates on
exact-match criteria [44]. Nearly 70 percent of campus-recruiting employers in a 2026 survey say they use
skills-based hiring [79]. The selection literature puts years of experience at .07 [1].

<!-- synthesis -->
The credential is a weak proxy and the announcement is not the practice: dropping a degree line changes hiring only
when the screen and the assessment change with it, and where they do, the non-degree hires stay longer [68] [44]
[1]. The sequence is a job analysis, a skill-based posting, an affirmative filter and a work sample or knowledge
test; where the customer's labor category requires the degree, the requirement is the customer's to lift [99].

The third, `pay-transparency`, asks whether posting laws, right-to-discuss protections and salary-history bans
raise pay, lower it, compress it or shift it between groups. Cullen and Pakzad-Hurson found that right-to-discuss
laws lowered wages by about 2 percent, because employers credibly refuse to pay any one worker more [90]. Arnold,
Quach and Taska found posting laws raised disclosure by about 30 percentage points and wages by 1.3 to 3.6 percent
[97]. Cullen's review sorts the effects by type: coworker transparency compresses and lowers wages, and cross-firm
transparency moves workers to higher-paying employers [91]. Card, Mas, Moretti and Saez showed that learning peers'
pay lowers satisfaction and raises job search below the unit median and does nothing above it [92]. Hansen and
McNichols found early salary-history bans raised the gender earnings ratio by about 1 percent [96].

<!-- synthesis -->
The posted range is cross-firm transparency, which raises what the firm has to pay by a few percent while raising
applicant counts; coworker transparency is the compression mechanism [97] [90] [91]. A firm that posts a defensible
range, states that pay is negotiable, reviews incumbents before each offer and sets pay from the job acts on all
five positions at once [92] [96]; what it cannot do is keep a new hire's pay from becoming the incumbents'
comparison point.

The fourth, `clearance-timeliness-figures`, asks which published timeline describes a sponsored contractor hire's
wait. The Performance Accountability Council's report for the third quarter of FY2026 gives government-wide figures
in the tens of days for moderate-risk cases and around a hundred for high-risk ones, against targets of 40 and 75
days [54]. DCSA's industry figures average 197 days for a Secret and 243 for a Top Secret and are not comparable
with the earlier 156 and 227 [126] [124] [125]. GAO found in December 2025 that 36 of 42 published timeliness
statistics were inaccurate, chiefly because DCSA's method understated processing time from 2020 to 2024 [56]. A
clearance practitioner adds that no public figure exists for how often or how fast a contractor applicant receives
an interim [127].

<!-- synthesis -->
The line to plan on is the DCSA industry figure for the current quarter, treated as a floor, because the
government-wide number mixes faster federal cases with industry's, the series changed basis, and GAO found the
older figures understated [126] [54] [56]. No start date that depends on an interim can be promised [127], and the
only phase the firm influences is initiation, about three weeks of the total [126].

The fifth, `who-pays-for-training`, asks what a firm that trains people in skills any employer can use should
expect to recover, and how. Standard theory, as Acemoglu and Pischke state it, has workers pay for general training
because they can take it anywhere; their own position is that firms pay because they know the trained worker's
ability better than rivals do, which gives them power over the wage after the fact [161]. Dearden, Reed and Van
Reenen found in British industry panels that a one-point rise in training went with about 0.6 percent more value
added per hour and 0.3 percent higher wages [159]. Manchester found at one large establishment that tuition
reimbursement raised retention, 80 percent of the effect coming through who sorted into the firm [160]. The
Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, with the California and Colorado legislatures, holds that repayment
provisions shift cost and risk to the worker and chill mobility whether or not enforced, and that the research it
cites puts their effect at about 15 percent less quitting [166] [167] [168].

<!-- synthesis -->
The investment is recovered through the productivity–wage wedge while the person stays and through the applicants
the offer of training attracts, not through a repayment clause, now unlawful in the strictest states for most
purposes [159] [160] [166] [167]. What extends the stay is pay that moves with capability and a next assignment;
what shortens it is a cleared, certified professional's outside market.

The sixth, `cost-of-turnover`, asks which replacement-cost figure to plan with: the 90 to 200 percent of salary in
practitioner literature, the case studies' median near a fifth, or the unit-level evidence counted in customers and
productivity. A trade-association white paper repeats direct costs of 50 to 60 percent and total costs of 90 to 200
percent and names no study [195]. Boushey and Glynn found a median of 21 percent of annual salary across 27 case
studies excluding executives and physicians, 16 percent for jobs paying $30,000 or less and up to 213 percent at
the top, with only 2 of 11 papers counting indirect costs [180]. Heavey, Holwerda and Hausknecht put unit turnover
at about −.22 with customer satisfaction and production efficiency and −.15 with profit margin [181]. Jäger and
Heining, using unexpected worker deaths in German records, found replacement costs largest for specialized skills
and in thin markets, where the firm also pays the remaining specialists more [193]. Allen, Bryant and Vardaman hold
that the practice literature carries misconceptions about turnover that the evidence does not support [179].

<!-- synthesis -->
The measured fifth of salary is the figure for ordinary replacements, counted with the indirect costs the case
studies left out [180]; the specialist whose market is thin is the exception the multiples were invented for, and
there the cost is the contract's capacity, the customer's satisfaction and the pay of everyone adjacent [181]
[193].

## 9. What changed in 2025 and 2026

The change log holds dated entries from 2021 onward, one of them, `ch-006`, kept and marked superseded because the
Colorado date it recorded never arrived [39]. The contractor EEO framework moved first. Executive Order 14173
revoked Executive Order 11246 on January 21, 2025, and three final rules published on August 21, 2026 remove its
implementing regulations effective October 26, 2026, narrow Section 503 to a $20,000 threshold with the 7 percent
utilization goal and self-identification solicitation rescinded, and update VEVRAA at a $200,000 threshold, both
from September 21, 2026 [86] [55]. Older contractor rules sit in the same group: the federal Fair Chance Act has
applied to contractors since December 20, 2021, barring criminal-history inquiries before a conditional offer, with
cleared and national-security positions excepted [48], and the August 2024 SkillBridge memorandum set a minimum
annual intake and a 75 percent qualifying-offer standard [74] [59].

The AI-screening rules moved in opposite directions at the federal and state levels. The EEOC removed its May 2023
technical assistance on algorithmic selection tools on January 27, 2025, while the Title VII rules it restated did
not change [35] [36]. California's regulations on automated-decision systems took effect on October 1, 2025,
requiring four-year record retention [40]. Illinois's amendment barring discriminatory AI use and requiring notice
took effect on January 1, 2026, and the clarifying rules proposed on May 15 were withdrawn on June 2 [38].

complaint by the Department of Justice, a stipulated order of April 27, 2026 barred its enforcement, and the
replacement signed on May 14 reenacts it from January 1, 2027 as a notice-based law, with notice before an
automated tool materially influences an employment decision, a description of the decision within 30 days of an
adverse outcome, meaningful human review, three-year records and Attorney General enforcement after a 60-day cure
notice [222] [221].

Pay transparency and salary history spread by statute. California's SB 642 clarified "pay scale" in postings from
January 1, 2026 [76]; Virginia, from July 1, 2026, requires a wage or salary range in every public and internal
posting and bans salary-history inquiries; Maine, from July 29, 2026, requires a range in every posting of an
employer with ten or more employees; Connecticut, from October 1, 2026, requires the wage or wage range and a

that require a security clearance among others [218].

Noncompetes and stay-or-pay terms went back to the states. The FTC acceded by a 3–1 vote on September 5, 2025 to
the vacatur of its Non-Compete Clause Rule [169], and the NLRB general counsel's memoranda treating stay-or-pay
terms as unlawful were rescinded, leaving the CFPB's position standing [168] [166]. California's AB 692 bars most
repayment obligations in contracts entered after January 1, 2026, with exceptions for transferable credentials and
for signing bonuses on stated conditions [167]. Tennessee voids noncompetes for workers earning under $70,000 from
July 1, 2026 [209]. Two laws are still ahead: Washington voids every noncompetition covenant from June 30, 2027

## 11. How the evidence was gathered, and how the author's guesses fared

<!-- figure: ledger-outcomes -->

<!-- framing -->
This guide began as a private matrix: sixty staffing trade-offs in eight categories, with seven observed failure points, written by its author from experience and described by him as best guesses in need of more rigor. None of it is cited here as fact. Each row, each category and each pattern became a hypothesis in a ledger, and the ledger was adjudicated against the sources read in seven stage sweeps, one per stage group, in the order select, attract, offer, clear and start, onboard and develop, retain and hand over, plan. An eighth sweep built the state overlay, confirmed the series behind the benchmark indicators and recorded the year's changes. The methods page publishes every query with its date and hit count, every adjudication with its citations, and every passage marked as synthesis.

<!-- framing -->
The ledger closed with twenty rows supported, forty-three supported with a qualification, four unstudied and none contradicted, and all eight categories reframed into the stages. A row is supported when a cited study or rule carries it; partly supported when it survives with a qualification the ledger states, which is most of them, because the author's rows were usually right about the direction and silent about the size; unstudied when the sweeps found nothing either way, which happened for agency fee levels, customer rejection feedback, single requisition ownership and customer approval cycles. The levers that carry those four practices say so in their own records. Figure 4 gives the count by category.

Every lever carries one of eight evidence grades, and the grade is the first thing to read on a lever page. A meta-analysis quotes its effect size with the k and N as reported, as the selection table does with structured interviews at .42 across pooled samples and the turnover table with withdrawal cognitions at .56 across 211 studies [1] [178]. A field experiment is a randomized or natural experiment in a real employment setting, such as the salary-database disclosure at one university [92] or the stated-preference experiment that priced working from home at eight percent of pay [115]. A quasi-experimental grade means a difference-in-differences or matched design on administrative data, as with the state posting laws [97] or the salary-history bans [96]. Observational studies, practitioner surveys, regulation and guidance follow, each named for what it is; the SHRM medians are survey facts with their publisher and year [196], and a regulation binds whether or not any study supports it.

<!-- synthesis -->
Three rules decided what the records could say. A secondary source, meaning a law-firm, consultant or vendor page, never carries a fact alone; it may point to a primary or flag a change, and where a gate still rests on one the record's conflicts line says so. A vendor figure with no primary behind it is logged and not carried, which is why the guide has no agency-fee figure and no weeks-to-productivity figure. A benchmark carries its reference period, its publisher and its publication date, because the figures move and are sometimes wrong at the source: the Government Accountability Office found in December 2025 that most of the clearance statistics it checked were unreliable, including a DCSA error that understated timelines from 2020 to 2024 [56], and DCSA then changed its reporting basis, which raised the published industry figures without any change in the process [126] [54].

The state overlay is the one place the secondary rule was relaxed, and the relaxation is marked on every row. Fifty-one records carry nine overlays each, from pay-range posting laws to mini-WARN thresholds, and almost every overlay rests on a dated compilation rather than the statute: the equal-pay roundup [207], the noncompete tracker [209], the stay-or-pay notes [211] [212], the AI chart [220], the cannabis page [219], the fair-chance guides [216] [217], the credit-check guide [218] and the mini-WARN chart [214]. Only Washington's two session laws [210] [215] and Colorado's fiscal note [222] were read as primaries. Each overlay carries a flag that says whether a primary is among its sources, the app shows the flag, and the statutes are the first item on the open list.

<!-- framing -->
What is still open is listed rather than hidden. Every reference is marked for a recheck against its live page, because the quotations came through a summarising fetch layer and none has yet been re-read in place. The cross-stage edges of the graph are incomplete: the sweeps drew the edges within each stage and the ninth sweep, which follows the first public build, adds the edges between stages, re-reads every ledger adjudication against the records as finally written, and clears the recheck flags. The guide is dated on its front page for that reason, and the changes page is where the next year's movement will be recorded first.
